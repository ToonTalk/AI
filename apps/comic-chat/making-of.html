THE MAKING OF WEB COMIC CHAT

How a 1996 Microsoft program that turned chat into comic strips
came back to life in a web browser — and what went wrong along the way.

Once upon a time: 1996

In 1996, Microsoft Research released a very strange and wonderful chat program. Instead of showing your conversation as lines of scrolling text, Comic Chat drew it — as a comic strip. You picked a character, typed a message, and the program decided everything else: who stood where, which way they faced, what expression they wore, and how the speech balloons should stack so their tails never crossed.

The characters were drawn by Jim Woodring, a celebrated underground cartoonist, in a scratchy woodcut style unlike anything else on a computer screen at the time. A little emotion wheel let you set your character's mood: the direction you clicked chose the feeling (happy, sad, angry, laughing…) and the distance from the center chose how strongly your character felt it.

And the lettering? The balloon font baked into the program is Comic Sans — a font designed at Microsoft in the same era, inspired by comic-book lettering. The world's most argued-about typeface is, in this one program, exactly where it belongs.

Thirty years later: the gift

In July 2026, Microsoft open-sourced Comic Chat under the MIT license — one of the most generous licenses there is. That meant the original program code and the original artwork could legally be studied, reused, and rebuilt, as long as Microsoft's copyright notice stays attached.

This project set out to do exactly that: rebuild Comic Chat as a single web page that runs in any browser, using the genuine artwork and the genuine rules — not a lookalike, but the real thing.

Act I: Faking it

The first version was built the way most tributes are: from memory and folklore. A brand-new cast of characters was drawn in code, and rules were invented that felt like Comic Chat — type in ALL CAPS and your character shouts, say “hello” and they wave, say “lol” and they laugh.

It worked, and it was fun. But it was an impression of the thing, not the thing. The obvious next step: the original program's own files were now sitting on the internet, free to read. Why guess?

Act II: Reading the blueprints

The released archive contains the program's original source code — thousands of lines of thirty-year-old C++. Reading it was like finding the architect's blueprints for a building you'd only known from postcards. And it turned out the postcards were wrong about several things everyone “remembers”:

  • Panels never varied in width. Every panel is an identical square, laid out 1 to 4 across. The “dynamic panel sizes” people remember are a trick of memory.
  • There is no shout balloon. Typing in caps changes your character's face, not the balloon. The spiky “shout bubble” in the first version of this port was pure invention, and it had to go.
  • Your own character got no special treatment. The feeling that you always stood on the same side of the panel comes from a subtle memory rule — the program gently penalises moving anyone away from where they stood last time — not from any privileged position.
  • Question marks do nothing. The program reacts to “lol”, to smileys, to sentences that start with “Hi” or “You” or “I” — but a “?” is just a way to end a sentence.

The rebuilt engine now follows the recovered rules exactly: the same balloon-stacking logic that keeps tails from ever crossing, the same random balloon widths (yes — the charmingly irregular balloons are deliberate randomness), the same close-up rule that crops a lone shouter's feet out of the frame.

Act III: Opening the toy box

The artwork was harder. All 31 characters live in files ending in .avb — a format nobody has read since the nineties, with no documentation. The decoder ring, fortunately, was in the box too: the program's own code for reading those files. Following it revealed a lovely design. Each character is a paper-doll kit: a set of heads (one per emotion, at different intensities) and a set of bodies (waving, pointing, shrugging…), with precise instructions for pinning any head onto any body. That's how “HI THERE!!!” can produce a character who is shouting with their face while waving with their arm — the two halves are chosen independently, exactly as the original did it.

All told, 557 poses were recovered across 31 characters — even more than the 25 characters mentioned in Microsoft's announcement, because a bonus art pack was hiding in the archive. Most of the cast is pure black-and-white woodcut, but three characters (Kirby, Buck and Veronica) are in full colour.

The paint-by-numbers disaster

Not everything went smoothly, and the best bug of the whole project deserves its own panel. The recovered images use a paint-by-numbers scheme: each dot in the picture is a number, and a little legend says which colour each number means. While repacking the art for the web, the numbers were shifted along by one — but the legend wasn't shifted with them. Every dot quietly became the next colour in the list.

For a character drawn in just black and white, “the next colour” means… the other one. Anna appeared in perfect photographic negative — black top, white skirt, glowing white hair. Bolo fared even worse: nearly all his ink landed on “white”, and he faded into a ghost.

Moral: when you copy a masterpiece, count the brushstrokes. After the fix, every repacked image is checked automatically — the amount of black ink going in must equal the amount coming out, to the last percent.

The striped countryside

The background scenes had their own trap. The archive contains two copies of each backdrop. The obvious ones — ordinary image files — turned out to be versions pre-striped for 1996 monitors, which could only show a handful of greys and faked the rest with fine horizontal lines. On a modern screen those baked-in stripes look like a broken television.

The real drawings — clean, wavy, hand-engraved hatching — were hiding in a newer set of files in the same locked format as the characters. One more decoding job, and the countryside got its trees back.

Small mysteries, solved by reading

Why was Kirby tiny? In an early build, poor Kirby stood waist-high to everyone. The answer was a confession in Microsoft's own code — a note the original programmers left for themselves, marked XXX (programmer for “we know, we know”), admitting the program simply assumes every character is the same height. Once the port made the same assumption, Kirby stood tall.

Epilogue: the cast learns to talk back

The newest chapter gives the characters a voice. A special version of the page can run inside claude.ai, where an AI can write each character's replies — in character. Anna answers in deadpan; Tiki issues volcanic pronouncements; Kirby thinks everything is THE BEST THING EVER. The AI doesn't just write the line: it also picks the emotion, how strongly to feel it, and whether the words belong in a speech balloon, a thought cloud, a whisper, or a stage direction — and the comic engine draws it like any other message.

Because the artwork is too big to carry into that environment, it travels as a separate suitcase: a single file you drop onto the page once, and the full original cast walks in.
